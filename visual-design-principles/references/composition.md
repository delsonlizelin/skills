# Composition across media

This reference adapts Ellen Lupton and Jennifer Cole Phillips, *Graphic Design: The New Basics*, second revised and expanded edition, into practical guidance. All citations below are **one-based PDF pages**, not printed folios. A **book claim** summarizes the authors' prose; an **application** is a decision method inferred for current work. Use the book as a set of ways to see and make relationships, not a mandate for a particular look. The examples span print, diagrams, presentations, and interfaces; adapt the tests to the medium, audience, content, and existing visual system.

## Explore the form before committing

**Book claim:** Formstorming is sustained visual thinking: multiple iterations help exhaust familiar responses and uncover less obvious ones. The hundred-iterations exercise deliberately varies how one subject is represented (PDF pp. 24, 26). **Application:** When the first layout looks polished but generic, sketch materially different arrangements before refining details. Change one underlying relationship at a time: focal position, density, image/text balance, crop, or sequence. For a frontend, compare a few viable content structures using actual data and states, then select the one that best supports the task. For a poster, explore more expressive directions. Do not require a literal hundred options for routine UI work; the exercise teaches range, while the number of alternatives should fit the problem. Check whether the chosen concept solves the communication need, not merely whether it looks novel.

## Find the underlying marks and fields

**Book claim:** Point, line, and plane combine into icons, textures, diagrams, patterns, animations, and typographic systems (PDF p. 66). **Application:** If a composition feels noisy, mentally reduce it to dots, paths, and areas of visual weight. Ask whether repeated lines create useful direction, whether large planes compete for dominance, and whether isolated points attract attention for a reason. In an interface, borders, dividers, card surfaces, icons, and text blocks all contribute to this structure. Remove a line if spacing or alignment already conveys the separation. In an expressive piece, dense marks may be exactly the intended texture; the goal is to understand their effect, not to erase them.

## Use color, texture, and pattern with a purpose

**Book claim:** Color can connect, distinguish, emphasize, hide, convey mood, or code information; its appearance changes in relation to surrounding colors and light (PDF p. 170). Texture can be physical or optical, helping establish mood, point of view, and a sense of material presence (PDF p. 145). Patterns can grow from simple units such as dots, stripes, and grids arranged in varied schemes (PDF p. 449). **Application:** If a colored region dominates without adding meaning, ask what relation its hue or contrast is meant to show; test it beside its neighbors, not as an isolated swatch. Use a tactile or optical texture when material character matters, and inspect whether it remains legible at the actual size. Build a pattern from a repeatable rule when recurrence itself carries the idea. A plain surface may serve a task better. These are optional tools, never a requirement to add color, grain, or ornament to every composition.

## Decide what the viewer encounters first

**Book claim:** Visual hierarchy conveys relative importance through scale, value, color, spacing, and placement; unclear levels make communication harder to navigate (PDF pp. 299–301). **Application:** State the desired reading order in plain language: first the main message or task, then the next useful choice, then supporting detail. If every title, image, badge, and action shouts equally, reduce competing emphasis before enlarging the main element. Use a small, consistent set of level changes: for example, stronger size and space for a section heading, quieter contrast and position for metadata. Test at the intended viewing size and scan quickly. A deliberately flat hierarchy can suit browsing, pattern, or ambiguity, but should be a conscious choice rather than a side effect of uniform styling. Interactive controls still need to be findable when the aesthetic favors restraint.

## Balance weight and create rhythm

**Book claim:** Balance comes from the proportion and placement of elements in relation to each other and the negative space. A design may balance asymmetrically; rhythm uses recurrence with change to guide attention through a static page or sequence (PDF pp. 99–100). The book calls symmetry inherently stable and asymmetry generally more active (PDF pp. 99, 101). **Application:** If the eye gets stuck in one corner, inspect the combined weight of size, darkness, color, detail, and surrounding space. Move, resize, or quiet an element before adding a counterweight. Repeat alignments, spacing intervals, and typographic roles to make a coherent path, then vary them to mark a meaningful event. A long article may need a steady reading rhythm; an exhibit graphic may use a sharper interruption. Symmetry might suit a ceremonial tone, while asymmetry may suit a more active one; these are contextual inferences. Review a sequence of pages or screens together: a single panel can be balanced while the overall experience feels monotonous or erratic.

## Judge scale in context

**Book claim:** Scale is both measurable size and the viewer's impression of size relative to the body, setting, and other objects. Some work must remain legible across widely different reproduction sizes (PDF p. 124). **Application:** When a logo, label, diagram, or call to action works only in the design canvas, test it at the smallest and largest real presentation sizes. Compare the size of the focal element with neighboring content, not just with a type-scale table. On a frontend, a hierarchy that works on a wide screen may collapse when a narrow viewport changes line breaks and distances. In print, viewing distance and physical placement matter. Oversized elements can create intimacy, urgency, or surprise; use them when that relationship advances the message, and verify that essential detail remains available.

## Design the ground as well as the figure

**Book claim:** Perception actively organizes fragments and separates forms from their surrounding ground; designers can use that process for direct communication or perceptual surprise (PDF p. 217). **Application:** If people may misread what belongs together, inspect the gaps, backgrounds, and repeated forms around the content. Related items should form a stronger visual group than unrelated ones through proximity, similarity, alignment, or a shared field. Check whether an empty area is acting as a margin, a pause, a separator, or an accidental hole. A graphic may intentionally ask the viewer to complete a missing shape; a form field or critical instruction usually benefits from a more immediate grouping. Test long labels, translated strings, and variable content so wrapping does not destroy the intended group.

## Use frames and layers to shape interpretation

**Book claim:** Cropping, borders, margins, captions, and surrounding settings frame how an image or object is understood (PDF p. 262). Layers can overlap while retaining distinct identities; maps, notation, printing, and digital media all use them (PDF p. 338). Transparency gains its effect through the relation between overlapping surfaces, and it may create ambiguity as well as depth (PDF p. 366). **Application:** When an image feels disconnected from its message, try a different crop or caption before changing the image. When many elements compete, group information into meaningful layers: base context, primary content, annotation, and action. Check the combined view, especially where translucent surfaces or text overlap detailed imagery. A full-bleed image may be powerful because it removes the protective margin; a border may improve comprehension by declaring a boundary. Use whichever relationship serves the content. Avoid adding depth simply because a tool makes it easy.

## Build a repeatable structure that permits variation

**Book claim:** A module is a fixed unit used in a larger system; constraints can generate many combinations (PDF p. 385). A grid aligns content and gives structure to margins and white space as well as occupied areas (PDF p. 415). In the book's nine-square color example, the repeated armature supports varied arrangements (PDF p. 420). **Application:** If a multipage or multi-screen project drifts visually, define a few stable relationships: content widths, alignment lines, spacing intervals, and recurring modules. Then allow content-led variation in span, density, or emphasis. A gallery may need a consistent browsing scaffold but different treatment for featured work; a report may need repeatable tables and flexible narrative pages. Do not turn the grid into equal-sized boxes for everything. The test is whether a new item feels related to the family while remaining clear on its own.

## Make diagrams and sequences explain something

**Book claim:** Diagrams use coded graphic relations to reveal structures, amounts, links, and change that may be difficult to see in a list or prose (PDF p. 485). Motion can be literal or implied, and both print sequences and animated media unfold in time (PDF p. 515). Rules can coordinate a family of outcomes without predetermining each one (PDF p. 549). **Application:** Before drawing a diagram, name the relationship it must make visible and assign each line, position, size, or color a stable meaning. Remove a decorative arrow if it suggests a causal or temporal link that is not there. Review the order in which a viewer encounters labels and evidence. In a frontend flow, inspect transitions among loading, empty, selected, and completed states; in a report or presentation, inspect the page-to-page reveal. Motion or sequential emphasis should clarify a change or direct attention. Static alternatives may communicate better when movement would distract.

## Verify the composition in its setting

The principles above are usable as written; the source PDF is provenance, not a dependency for applying this reference. Exact figure details are a separate matter: text extraction cannot preserve their colors, alignments, crops, or diagram logic, and many reproduced artworks have no extractable text layer. Inspect the work being designed at its intended size and in sequence. Record the observed symptom, the relationship causing it, the change made, and what the revised artifact visibly improves. These checks are an application of the principles above, not experimental proof that viewers comprehend the work faster. When a chosen style or established design system constrains the available marks, keep the communication aim and find a compatible treatment.
